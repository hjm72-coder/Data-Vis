Youth Sports

What the U.S. World Cup Exit Revealed About Pay-to-Play

How the commercialization of youth sports decides who gets to dream — and who gets priced out.

By JoJo McShane • August 9, 2026

The price tag starts before a child can even tie their own cleats.

As the U.S. reassesses its soccer pipeline in the wake of the 2026 World Cup, the financial barrier to entry is showing up earlier than ever — starting with elementary school-aged players.

In affluent suburbs, families are paying as much as $3,500 in club fees for nine-year-olds, according to CNN. A father in Tennessee, who asked not to be named for fear his sons' clubs would treat them differently, estimates he spends $15,000 a year on soccer for his two boys. His reasoning is blunt: “Money makes a difference in how good your kid is.”

Over the last two decades, youth sports in America have quietly morphed from a community pastime into a $40 billion industry, according to the Aspen Institute's Project Play. And as the money has flowed in, the doorway to athletic opportunity has narrowed to those who can afford it.

The price of participation

The average U.S. sports family spent $1,016 on a single child's primary sport in 2024 — a 46% increase since 2019, according to Project Play. Across all of a child's sports activities, families paid nearly $1,500 annually per child.

The increases cut across the most popular sports: spending on basketball jumped 105% over five years, soccer 69%, and baseball 68%.

But the averages mask a deeper divide. The wealthiest families spent $1,471 more per year on a child's primary sport than families earning under $50,000, Project Play found. Children from lower-income households were three times less likely to play on traveling teams than their wealthier peers.

“Youth sports inflation is out of control and no segment of the population is untouched. Parents will spend just about anything for their children but when more money is being wrung out of fewer families, we're leaving a lot of opportunity on the table — denying many kids the benefits of sports.” Tom Farrey, executive director of the Aspen Institute's Sports & Society Program

The cost beyond the fee

The biggest single line item is no longer registration or equipment; it's travel. Travel and lodging averaged roughly $280 per sport, per child, in 2024, making it the largest spending category in the Project Play survey. Private lessons and instruction added another $165 per sport per child.

“League costs are not what's really impacting most parents. It's the travel. For a lot of parents, it's not just the team. Many will get into private coaching and they'll start to get into all of the add-ons that they feel they need.” Skip Gilbert, former CEO of US Youth Soccer

Access becomes the advantage

The quiet injustice at the center of the pay-to-play model: it doesn't simply reward talent. It rewards access. According to the recruiting service Next College Student Athlete, roughly 90% of college-recruited athletes participated in club sports.

If the path to college runs through the club system, and the club system costs thousands, then the path to college is, in practice, a paywall.

For Heather Maddocks, a freshman playing for New England Futbol Club, the bill is around $5,000. “There's also bags, uniforms, the whole NEFC kit, and they don't cover plane tickets or hotel fees,” she said.

The financial burden carries an emotional tax. For 15-year-old Makenna Johnson, a soccer player on a travel team ranked third in Georgia and 22nd nationally, the stakes are inseparable from her family's finances. Her mother estimates she spends $10,000 to $12,000 a year on soccer.

“We do not have money like these other people. It affects us financially more than other people. We came too far. The stakes are much more for us. You cannot blow this opportunity.” Meliesha Johnson, speaking to CNN

Who gets seen

The U.S. system's costs shape who gets visible to recruiters and top clubs. “If you want to be considered, to be seen by some of the best, you've got to put yourself in that environment,” said Jeffrey Golston, who coached Makenna at TopHat.
